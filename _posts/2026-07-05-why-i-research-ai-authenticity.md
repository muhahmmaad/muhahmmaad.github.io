Three of my four papers orbit the same question. When an AI system displays
social traits — warmth, humour, apparent empathy — how does that shape what
people believe about it, and what they go on to buy?

## The mediating variable is the interesting part

The naive model says social traits increase purchase intention directly. The
data doesn't really support that. What the evidence points to is a mediator:
**perceived authenticity**. Social traits raise how genuine the system seems,
and that perception is what moves intention.

That distinction matters, because it means the effect is fragile. Authenticity
perceptions collapse when people detect that warmth is engineered. A chatbot
that seems friendly helps; one that is *caught performing* friendliness costs
more than a neutral one would have.

## Why a marketer should care

I sit on both sides of this. Professionally I run campaigns that use these
tools. Academically I keep finding that the trust they generate is easier to
spend than to rebuild.

The practical version: use AI to be faster and more responsive, not to fake a
relationship. The first is an efficiency gain. The second is a loan against
your credibility.

## What's still open

My current paper — a model of AI social attributes and their influence on
perceived authenticity and purchase intention — is under review. The gap I
haven't closed is longitudinal: nearly all of this work measures a single
interaction. What happens to authenticity perceptions over months of exposure
is, as far as I can tell, still unanswered.
